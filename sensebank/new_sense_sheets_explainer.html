Background

The PDT annotators tagged words with senses of their own copy of the Czech WordNet. When those tags are carried over to the Czech WordNet 2.2, some senses are not there. A sense here is a lemma with the synset it was tagged with: moci in the ÚFAL synset NAS-450034833 ("possibility"), mít in NAS-430683854 ("have to"), znát in 00334470-v. The script sorts them into four reasons the release lacks them:

ReasonIn the release?SensesJudgedExample
word not in the wncze synsetthe concept, without this word98860znát, 43 tags
concept not in wnczeno synset for the ILI8080premiér, voda
new synset (no Princeton id)a synset ÚFAL or Brno added5353moci, mít, říkat
no ILI for the 1.5 offsetCILI has no link1717stranický

The first reason is easy to fix: add the word to a synset that exists. So only a sample of 60 is judged, to measure how often adding the word would be right. The other three are judged in full. Counts are from report/facts-sheets.json after the run at commit df27b6f.

Why judge each sense twice? One judge gives a decision; two give a decision and a measure of how reliable decisions are. Cohen's kappa compares the judges' agreement with the agreement chance alone would give. With seven students, any two of them form a pair, and there are 7×6/2 = 21 pairs: A–B, A–C, … F–G.

And why random? Three things would bias the results if they were left to the order of a file: which senses are judged, who judges which, and what each judge sees first. The script draws all three at random, from a fixed seed so the draw can be repeated exactly.

Now sort the 210 senses from most to least tagged and deal them like cards, 21 at a time. Before each round the 21 pairs are shuffled, so who gets the first card of a round is random. The first round of the real run went:

moci 1183 → B–E mít 902 → A–G moci 240 → E–G říkat 191 → B–G … 17 more

Because every round gives each pair exactly one sense, and the rounds walk down the frequency list, every pair ends with one sense from the top band, one from the next, and so on. Two real pairs, their senses' tag counts:

A–B  67, 18, 6, 5, 3, 3, 2, 1, 1, 1
B–E  1183, 14, 9, 5, 4, 2, 2, 1, 1, 1

Both pairs judge a frequent sense and several rare ones, so neither pair's kappa rests only on easy, well-attested senses. Then three more shuffles: the item numbers, the order of each sheet, and the example sentences. The key that undoes them all is items.tsv; its first rows:

2 · rng(): one seeded stream per purpose

new_sense_sheets.py:111–120

Every random step asks for its own generator, seeded with the seed and a purpose name: "sample", "assign", "number", "examples", and "order-A"…"order-G". Python's random.seed accepts a string and turns it into the same state on every run, so rng("assign") always deals the same way. Separate streams mean that sampling one more example sentence cannot change who judges what.

def rng(purpose: str) -> random.Random:
    return random.Random(f"{SEED}-{purpose}")

3 · collect(): find the senses, sample the easy ones

new_sense_sheets.py:123–150

Every annotated word goes through fate() from pdt_frequencies.py, so the sheets use exactly the rule the frequency counts use. Words whose fate is one of the four reasons in MISSING are grouped by (PDT synset, lemma), keeping every sentence. Then each reason is sorted (so the sample does not depend on file order) and sampled to its quota; reasons without a quota keep everything.

for reason in sorted(MISSING):
    mine = sorted((i for i in items.values() if i.reason == reason),
                  key=lambda i: (i.pdt_synset, i.lemma))
    POPULATION[reason] = len(mine)
    quota = QUOTA.get(reason, len(mine))
    keep += r.sample(mine, min(quota, len(mine)))

Why uniform? Every one of the 988 senses has the same chance of being among the 60. So the share the students judge right is an unbiased estimate of the share right among all 988: the precision of adding them in bulk. Picking the 60 most frequent would answer a different question. The sample happens to hold 24 senses tagged once and 11 tagged ten times or more.

4 · assign(): deal by frequency to shuffled pairs

new_sense_sheets.py:153–165

The items arrive most tagged first. They are dealt in rounds of 21, and each round shuffles a fresh copy of the pairs. 210 items make exactly ten rounds, so every pair gets ten senses and every student sixty.

pairs = list(combinations(STUDENTS, 2))
r = rng("assign")
for start in range(0, len(items), len(pairs)):
    order = pairs[:]
    r.shuffle(order)
    for item, pair in zip(items[start:start + len(pairs)], order):
        item.judges = pair

5 · number() and sample_sentences()

new_sense_sheets.py:168–189

number() shuffles a copy of the items and numbers them S001, S002, … in that random order. sample_sentences() keeps up to three distinct sentences per sense, sampled from all of them, so the commonest phrasing does not crowd out the rest; both judges of a sense see the same three.

6 · The row a student reads

pdt_sense() gives the PDT synset's words, definition and examples, and its English through the ILI; wncze_senses() lists the senses the release already has for the lemma (or its reflexive forms), numbered, with English glosses from omw-en:2.0. A real row of sheet A:

Here the student must decide whether "a chocolate sweet" is a sense the release lacks (add) or the same as sense 1, chocolate the food (already in wncze). One sentence is little to go on, which is what unsure is for.

7 · write_sheet(): one workbook per student

Each workbook, written with openpyxl, has an instructions sheet and a judge sheet. The decision column gets a drop-down, so answers come back in exactly four spellings and can be scored by a script.

8 · main(): shuffle each sheet, write the key and the facts

new_sense_sheets.py:281–323

Each sense's row is appended to both its judges' sheets, then every sheet is shuffled with its own stream, so the two judges of one sense meet it at different points and tiredness does not hit both judgements at once. The key, items.tsv, holds what the sheets hide: the reason, the tag count and the judges. The counts go to report/facts-sheets.json, which the report cites.

for student in STUDENTS:
    rows = sheets[student]
    rng(f"order-{student}").shuffle(rows)
    write_sheet(student, rows)

How much to trust this

The code excerpts are copied from new_sense_sheets.py at commit df27b6f, and the line references were checked against that file with explainer_check.py. The tables and the sheet row come from the run's own outputs (data/sheets/items.tsv, sheet-A.xlsx, report/facts-sheets.json), and the first-round deals were reproduced by re-seeding rng("assign"). If the seed, quota or students change, every number here changes with them.

Glossary

sensea lemma with the synset it was tagged with: moci in NAS-450034833.
pairtwo of the seven students; there are 21, and each judges ten senses together.
Cohen's kappaagreement between two judges, corrected for the agreement chance would give; 1 is perfect, 0 is chance.
seedthe starting value of a random generator; the same seed gives the same draws, so the sheets can be rebuilt.
stratifieddealt so that every group (here, every pair) gets a share of each band (here, of frequency).
blindthe sheets hide the tag count and the reason, so they cannot sway the judgement.
keyitems.tsv: what the sheets hide, used to score them afterwards.
