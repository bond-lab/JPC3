Background

new_sense_sheets.py dealt 210 PDT senses that the Czech WordNet 2.2 lacks to seven students, A to G. Every sense went to two of them, and every pair of students shares about ten. Each student chose one of four answers per sense:

AnswerMeaning
adda real sense of the word that the wordnet is missing
already in wnczethe wordnet has it; the student gives its number
wrong tagthe sentences do not use the word in the PDT sense
unsurecannot decide; the comment says why

Two judges per item give two things. Where they agree, the answer is better founded than one person's. Where they disagree, the item goes back for discussion. Across many items, how often they agree measures how reliable the whole exercise is.

Raw agreement flatters, though. If both students answered add to four items in five without reading them, they would agree on about two thirds of the items by chance alone. Kappa corrects for that: it is the share of the possible agreement beyond chance that the judges actually reach.

κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe)

po is the share of items the judges agree on; pe the share they would agree on by chance, given how often each of them uses each answer. 1 is perfect agreement, 0 is chance, and below 0 is worse than chance. Two versions matter here:

  • Cohen's kappa is for the same two judges throughout. It fits one pair of students, A and B, on the ten items they share.
  • Fleiss' kappa allows the judges to change from item to item, as long as each item has the same number of them. It fits the whole set: every item has two judges, but a different two.

They agree on 7 of 10, so po = 0.70. By chance, they would both say add with probability 6/10 × 7/10, both already 1/10 × 1/10, both wrong 2/10 × 2/10, and both unsure 1/10 × 0/10. Together that is pe = 0.47. So:

κ = (0.70 − 0.47) / (1 − 0.47) = 0.43

That is the value statsmodels returns for this table (computed with the same call the script makes). 70% agreement sounds good. Kappa says it is less than half of the way from chance to perfect, because add is so common that the two would often agree on it anyway.

Ten items is a small sample. One changed answer moves this kappa by more than 0.1. A pair's kappa is for spotting a pair that reads the task differently (a kappa near 0, or below), not for ranking students. The figure to report is Fleiss' kappa over all items.

Each item ends with one outcome: the agreed answer, split if the two differ, or pending while a sheet is still out. The report counts outcomes for each reason the release lacks a sense; the splits go to data/scores/split.tsv to be resolved.

1 · Answer and Item: what is stored

An Answer is one student's decision, the sense number for already in wncze, and a comment. An Item is one sense from the key, its two judges, and the answers returned so far. Its outcome is a property, worked out when asked, so it is always up to date with the answers.

@property
def outcome(self) -> str:
    if len(self.answers) < 2:
        return PENDING
    first, second = (self.answers[j].decision for j in self.judges)
    return first if first == second else SPLIT

5 · fleiss() and cohen(): agreement beyond chance

score_sheets.py:164–203

Both turn answers into numbers (the index of the answer in CHOICES) and hand the counting to statsmodels, which implements both kappas. fleiss() builds one row per item, the two judges' answers, and aggregate_raters() turns that into how many judges chose each answer. cohen() builds the 4×4 table of the Intuition section for one pair.

table, _ = aggregate_raters(np.array(rated), n_cat=len(CHOICES))
return round(float(fleiss_kappa(table)), 2)

Kappa is undefined when chance agreement is already 1, for instance when both students answered add to every item they share: then 1 − pe is 0. cohen() checks for that and returns None; the pair is left out of the facts rather than reported as a misleading 0.

chance = (table.sum(axis=1) @ table.sum(axis=0)) / n**2
if chance == 1:
    return None

7 · main(): the report's figures and the files to act on

Summarises all items and each of the four reasons, computes a kappa for each of the 21 pairs, and for the senses where only the word was missing the share judged add.
